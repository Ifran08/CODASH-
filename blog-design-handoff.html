Why we stopped handing off design files

FEATURED · SEP 2026

Most web projects follow the same pattern. A designer builds the look in Figma, exports a set of files, and hands them to a developer who has to interpret every choice from scratch. Somewhere in that handoff, details get lost. Spacing shifts. Interactions get simplified because nobody documented how they should behave. The site that ships is close to the design, but not quite it.

We used to work this way too, split between a design phase and a build phase with a formal handoff in between. It worked fine on paper. In practice, it meant every project had a point where quality quietly dropped, right when the design met the code.

The real cost of a handoff

A handoff isn't just a file transfer. It's a translation, and translations lose meaning. A developer looking at static frames has to guess at things the designer knew instinctively: how a card should feel on hover, what happens when text runs longer than expected, how a layout should behave on a phone with an unusual screen size.

None of these guesses are wrong exactly. They're just different from what the designer had in mind, and those small differences add up to a site that feels slightly off from the vision that sold the client in the first place.

What changed for us

We now build design and development as one continuous process, done by people who talk to each other constantly instead of passing files over a wall. The person shaping the layout is in the same room, sometimes the same person, as the one writing the code that brings it to life.

That means decisions that used to get made twice, once in the design tool and once in the browser, now get made once. It means when something looks great in a mockup but turns out clunky to build responsively, we catch it in minutes instead of after the client has already approved it.

The best websites aren't designed and then built. They're designed while being built.

What this means for clients

In practice, working with one team through the whole build means fewer surprises. What you approve early on is much closer to what actually ships, because the people building it were part of shaping it. It also means faster turnarounds, since there's no waiting for a separate team to pick up a project and get up to speed.

It's a big part of why we describe ourselves as full stack rather than as a design studio or a development shop. The layers aren't separate departments here. They're one process, start to finish.
